Backpropagation: a step-by-step tutorial for beginners

The Blame Chain

A network makes a bad guess. Before it can improve, it has to figure out exactly which of its internal connections deserve the blame and by how much. Here is that process—call backpropagation (or backward propagation of errors) — clearly presented one step at a time.

Picture a very small chain of command: two input units at the start, two hidden units in the middle, and one output unit at the end that makes the final call. A signal flows forward through this chain, and out comes a prediction.

Now suppose the prediction is wrong. There is a gap between the network's desired output and its actual output. The only way to find out where responsibility lies for the mistake is to start at the end, where it is visible, and work backward, asking each unit in turn: how much did you contribute to the error? That backward hand-off of blame, one layer at a time, is the essence of backpropagation.

Below, we'll run that process on one tiny, fully-labeled network, using simple arithmetic and a few pre-derived formulas. No calculus background is needed to follow along — wherever a slope or a rate of change would normally require it, we've pre-derived the simple formula for you, so every number is checkable on a phone calculator.

Six words you'll need
weight
A number sitting on a connection between two units, loosely modeled on the strength of a biological synapse. See “what a weight really is,” just below.
activation
The number a unit reports after squashing its raw input through the sigmoid function — always between 0 and 1. See “activation and the sigmoid function,” just below.
error
The difference between the network's output and the target. In our calculations, we keep track of its sign because the sign determines the direction of the update.
sensitivity (slope)
How steep a unit's S-shaped output curve is exactly where that unit currently sits. The backpropagated signal passing through a unit gets multiplied by this — steep means the signal counts in full, flat means the unit barely reacts to it.
gradient
The derivative of the loss with respect to a particular weight. Its sign gives the direction in which changing that weight would increase the loss.
learning rate
A dial controlling how big a step the network takes when it applies a gradient. We'll use 0.001, a commonly used default.

A closer look: what a weight really is

Artificial neural networks (ANNs) are conceptually and mathematically inspired by the interconnected networks of biological neurons found in the brain. In computer science, we use artificial "units" (neurons) that process data, "weights" (loosely analogous to synaptic strengths) that scale signals, and "activations" (loosely analogous to levels of neural activity) that pass between layers. These are useful analogies, not literal biological equivalents. To understand how information and "blame" flow through artificial networks, it helps to look briefly at their natural inspiration.

Biological Neurons: Inhibitory vs. Excitatory Connections
Artificial Neural Network: Summation of Inputs

Real neurons connect to each other through synapses, and how much activity in one neuron (the presynaptic neuron, the one sending the signal) affects the neuron on the receiving end (the postsynaptic neuron) depends on the strength and type of that synapse. Some synapses are excitatory: activity in the presynaptic neuron pushes the postsynaptic neuron toward firing. Others are inhibitory: activity in the presynaptic neuron pushes the postsynaptic neuron away from firing, damping it down instead.

A weight in this network is a simplified stand-in for that synaptic strength. A positive weight acts like an excitatory synapse — the more active the sending unit is, the more it pushes the receiving unit toward turning on. A negative weight acts like an inhibitory synapse — the more active the sending unit is, the more it pushes the receiving unit toward turning off. A weight near zero barely functions as a synapse at all: however active the sending unit gets, almost none of that activity reaches the receiving unit.

Our example network mixes both kinds. i₁'s connections into the hidden layer (weights 0.10 and 0.20) are excitatory: the more active i₁ is, the more it pushes h₁ and h₂ toward firing. i₂'s connections into the hidden layer (weights −0.30 and −0.40) are inhibitory instead — activity in i₂ actually pushes both hidden units away from firing, damping them down rather than encouraging them. The two connections from the hidden layer into the output (weights 0.50 and 0.90) are both excitatory, with h₂'s connection (0.90) a much stronger push than h₁'s (0.50), the same way a stronger real synapse drives its target neuron more forcefully. When later steps talk about “correcting a weight,” this is literally what's being adjusted: exactly how excitatory — or, as with i₂'s connections, how inhibitory — a particular connection should be, given the mistake the network just made.

A closer look: activation and the sigmoid function

Real neurons don't report a smooth number between 0 and 1 — they either fire an electrical spike or they don't, and what varies is how often. Each unit in this network is a simplified stand-in for that: instead of a firing rate, it reports one number, its activation, somewhere between 0 (completely silent) and 1 (firing as hard as it can).

To get that number, a unit first adds up everything arriving at it — every incoming unit's activation, multiplied by the weight of that particular connection — into one raw total. Then it runs that raw total through a fixed squashing rule called the sigmoid function, written sigmoid(x) = 1 ÷ (1 + e−x), where x stands for whatever raw total is being squashed. The sigmoid function takes any number at all, however large or however negative, and maps it onto a value strictly between 0 and 1. That's the S-shaped curve below. In this standalone example, the unit's raw total input is 1.50 and its output (activation) after passing through the sigmoid function is 0.82.

the math — computing one activation
sigmoid(x)=1 ÷ (1 + e−x)
raw input (weighted sum)=1.50
e−1.50≈0.2231
1 + 0.2231=1.2231
1 ÷ 1.2231=0.82 ← the activation

e here is just a fixed constant, about 2.718; e−x is a single button (often labeled ex) on any scientific calculator, or one search away on your phone. You won't need to run this calculation again. In the network example below, however, we will actually check the forward-pass arithmetic so that the displayed activations match the displayed inputs and weights. This is just so you've seen, once, where numbers like that actually come from.

Notice how the curve behaves. Near the middle, it's steep: a small change in raw input produces a big swing in activation. Toward either end, it flattens: even a large change in raw input barely moves the activation at all, because the unit is already close to fully off or fully on. That difference in steepness from point to point is exactly what we'll call a unit's sensitivity — how much a unit's activation would actually respond, right now, to a change in what's feeding into it.

Meet the network

Here is our cast, mid-training. A forward pass has already run once. The two input values and the six weights below are the starting values for our worked example; the hidden and output activations are then computed from them using the sigmoid function above. The blue numbers are all known before we start; nothing here is a mystery yet.

This example's target is 1.00. Suppose the task is “does this photo contain a face” — a target of 1.00 means a face really is present in this training photo, confirmed by whoever labeled the data; a target of 0.00 would mean a face is confirmed absent. The network's job is to eventually output something close to 1.00 whenever a face really is there, and something close to 0.00 whenever it isn't. Right now, on this particular example, it outputs 0.6668 when it should output something close to 1.00.

To check the numbers: h₁ receives 1.00 × 0.10 + 0.50 × (−0.30) = −0.05, so sigmoid(−0.05) ≈ 0.4875. h₂ receives 1.00 × 0.20 + 0.50 × (−0.40) = 0, so sigmoid(0) = 0.5000. The output then receives 0.4875 × 0.50 + 0.5000 × 0.90 ≈ 0.6938, giving sigmoid(0.6938) ≈ 0.6668.

a note on biases
Real units usually carry one more adjustable number besides their incoming weights: a bias, added to the raw input before it's squashed through the sigmoid function. You can think of a bias as a constant numerical value added to the input of a unit that does not change as the result of incoming activations. We've left biases out of this walkthrough to keep the arithmetic to a single, easy-to-follow pipeline, but they aren't handled any differently: each unit's bias gets updated using that same unit's error —represented using the Greek letter delta (δ) — exactly like every weight we compute below, just without an incoming activation to multiply by.
1

Calculate the output error

Everything starts at the very end, because that's the only place the network can check its work. It compares its final guess to the true target.

Our output unit predicted 0.6668 — roughly a 67% probability according to the network. The target was 1.00: a face is present in this training example. The gap between the prediction and target is 1.00 − 0.6668 = 0.3332. For the gradient calculation, however, we need the signed difference between the output and the target: 0.6668 − 1.00 = −0.3332. The negative sign matters because the prediction needs to move upward. This subtraction happens at the output unit specifically, because the output unit is the only place in the network where we have both a prediction and a known correct answer to compare it against — the hidden and input units have no such comparison available to them.

2

Fix the connections into the output

The primary goal of this step is to calculate the output unit's backpropagated error signal (written here as δo). Before we can calculate the gradients of the two connections feeding directly into the output unit — the ones from h₁ and h₂ — we have to account for how responsive the output neuron is to changes in its raw input. The signed output error from Step 1 is −0.3332.

Recall from the sigmoid function: every unit's activation comes from squashing its raw input through an S-shaped curve. Because this curve isn't equally steep everywhere, a neuron's responsiveness changes depending on where it sits. To understand why this matters, think of the sigmoid curve as a hill:

  • At the midpoint (raw input = 0, activation = 0.5): The hill is a sheer cliff. The neuron is highly sensitive; a tiny nudge to the weights causes a large, dramatic jump in the output.
  • At the flat edges (near 0 or 1): The hill flattens into a plateau. The neuron has low sensitivity; tweaking the weights will barely change the final altitude (activation) at all.

Our output unit's activation is 0.6668, meaning it sits somewhat beyond the steep midpoint, as pictured below. The steepness (the slope) right at that point tells us exactly how much the activation would move if we adjusted its weights — critical information we must factor in before computing a correction.

Because the sigmoid curve has a convenient mathematical property, we can find its slope directly from its current output value using the formula: activation × (1 − activation). This lets us calculate the local derivative without manually differentiating the sigmoid each time.

For our output unit, the calculation is 0.6668 × (1 − 0.6668) ≈ 0.2222. This is below the maximum sigmoid slope of 0.25. We multiply the signed output error by this slope to obtain the backpropagated error signal used below:

  • Signed Output Error × Slope = δo ≈ −0.3332 × 0.2222 = −0.0740

This number — −0.0740 — is our completed backpropagated error signal (δo). Every subsequent calculation downstream uses this signed value. Its negative sign tells us that the weights feeding the output should increase, because the network needs a larger output.

Now that we have our sensitivity-adjusted error (δo), we can use it to calculate the actual corrections for the weights feeding into the output unit. However, we should be precise about what these “corrections” actually are. They are not the new weights themselves, and they are not percentage changes. They are called weight gradients, and each one packs two crucial pieces of information into a single number:

  • The Sign (Direction): The gradient points in the direction that increases the loss. Therefore, a positive gradient means increasing the weight would increase the loss, so gradient descent will decrease the weight. A negative gradient means increasing the weight would decrease the loss, so gradient descent will increase the weight.
  • The Magnitude (Scale): Its size tells us roughly how much that particular weight matters to the current mistake, relative to every other weight being corrected in this same pass.

Concretely, we calculate a separate gradient for each connection tracing back to the output unit:

  • For the connection from h₁: δo × h₁'s activation ≈ −0.0740 × 0.4875 = −0.0361
  • For the connection from h₂: δo × h₂'s activation ≈ −0.0740 × 0.5000 = −0.0370

These two separate computations share the same δo simply because both connections feed into the same output unit's error. Nothing gets applied yet, though—every gradient in the network must be computed first across all six steps. Only in Step 6 do we actually use them to update the weights.

3

Send the error back to the hidden layer

This is the heart of backpropagation. The hidden units never saw a "true target" — they have nothing to compare themselves against. So we can't compute their error the way we did in Step 1. Instead, we work out how much each hidden unit is responsible for the output's mistake, based entirely on the strength of its connection to the output.

We take δo, the output's backpropagated error signal from Step 2 (the one that already has the output's sigmoid slope folded in) and multiply it by the weight of the connection linking each hidden unit to the output:

  • For h₁: δo × weight(h₁→o) ≈ −0.0740 × 0.50 = −0.0370
  • For h₂: δo × weight(h₂→o) ≈ −0.0740 × 0.90 = −0.0666

This mirrors the forward pass in reverse: going forward, a hidden unit's activation reached the output multiplied by exactly this same weight, so running that same multiplication backward is a fair way to decide how much of the output's mistake traces back to that hidden unit. A hidden unit with a strong connection to a badly-wrong output is assigned a large share of the error — as h₂ is here, with a magnitude of about 0.0666 against h₁'s 0.0370. A hidden unit with a weak or near-zero connection contributed almost nothing to that mistake in the forward pass — and is assigned almost none of it back now.

4

Account for each hidden unit's sensitivity

Being assigned a share of the error doesn't automatically mean a unit is easy to correct. Each hidden unit sits on that same sigmoid curve we've been using all along, just at its own particular point on it — h₁ at 0.4875, h₂ at 0.5000. Using the same slope shortcut as before, h₁'s slope is 0.4875 × (1 − 0.4875) ≈ 0.2498, and h₂'s slope is 0.5000 × (1 − 0.5000) = 0.25. Where the curve is steep, a small nudge to the incoming weights produces a big change in the unit's activation. Where the curve is flat — the unit is pinned near 0 or near 1, fully "on" or fully "off" — nudging its weights barely moves anything.

Put another way: the error we computed in Step 3 tells us how much a hidden unit's activation should change to help fix the output's mistake. The slope we compute here tells us how much that activation would actually change in response to a change in the unit's own inputs.

Multiplying the two together—exactly the trick we already used in Step 2 on the output unit—combines “how much should this change” with “how much would this actually change” into one number. Using that same shortcut, we get each hidden unit's final, sensitivity-adjusted error:

  • For h₁: −0.0370 × 0.2498 ≈ δh1 = −0.0092
  • For h₂: −0.0666 × 0.25 ≈ δh2 = −0.0167

Notice what happened here: h₂ receives a larger-magnitude backpropagated signal because its connection to the output is stronger (0.90 vs. 0.50). Both hidden units, however, are near the sigmoid midpoint, so their local slopes are close to the maximum of 0.25. The sensitivity adjustment therefore changes the magnitudes only modestly. In a saturated unit near 0 or 1, the same adjustment would shrink the signal much more strongly.

5

Fix the connections into the hidden layer

We finally reach the first set of connections in the network — the ones running from the two input units into the two hidden units. The pattern is exactly the one from Step 2, just one layer further back: multiply each hidden unit's sensitivity-adjusted error by the activation of the input unit feeding that connection. This gives us the weight gradient for each of the four connections between the input layer and the hidden layer. Recall that the activation for i₁ = 1.00 and the activation for i₂ = 0.50. This gives us the following connection calculations:

  • For Hidden Unit h₁ (δh1 ≈ −0.0092):
    • Gradient for i₁→h₁: δh1 × i₁'s activation = −0.0092 × 1.00 = −0.0092
    • Gradient for i₂→h₁: δh1 × i₂'s activation = −0.0092 × 0.50 = −0.0046
  • For Hidden Unit h₂ (δh2 ≈ −0.0167):
    • Gradient for i₁→h₂: δh2 × i₁'s activation = −0.0167 × 1.00 = −0.0167
    • Gradient for i₂→h₂: δh2 × i₂'s activation = −0.0167 × 0.50 = −0.0083

The logic here mirrors Step 2 perfectly: a connection can only be blamed and corrected in proportion to how much signal was actually flowing through it during the forward pass. If an input unit's activation were 0.00, it would have sent no signal down that pathway at all — meaning it played no part in this specific mistake, and its gradient would correctly evaluate to 0. An input unit with a strong activation, by contrast, pushed a heavy signal down its paths, and receives a correspondingly larger correction gradient.

6

Take the collective step

The chain reaction has now reached the beginning of the network. Every single connection — all six in our toy example, or hundreds of thousands in a real production model — has a gradient value attached to it. This tells us how the loss changes when that specific connection changes, and therefore which direction gradient descent should move it.

Only now does gradient descent actually modify the network. It takes every single gradient we calculated, scales it down by our learning rate (our step-size dial, set here to a standard default of 0.001), and nudges every weight in the network at the same time. Nothing moves before this point; nothing is skipped after it.

One thing worth flagging if you ever see backpropagation written out in code (in a library like PyTorch, for instance): it is typically written as subtracting the scaled gradient. Because the gradient points in the direction of increasing loss, we must subtract it to push the weights downward toward minimum error. The standard update rule is:

New Weight = Old Weight − (Learning Rate × Gradient)

Why scale the gradient down at all, instead of just subtracting it straight from each weight? Because a gradient is only trustworthy for a very small step. It is calculated entirely from the network's current state, and if that state changes too much in one massive move, the gradient's advice is no longer accurate — it was describing a network state that no longer exists. Multiplying by a small learning rate keeps each update conservative: enough to make steady, real progress, but not so much that the network overshoots a good solution. This is also where the name comes from — the network is descending toward lower error in small, controlled steps, not leaping there in one.

Let's look at how all six connections across our network layers update simultaneously using this universal format:

  • Output Layer Connections (Feeding into Unit o)
    • For h₁→o (Old weight = 0.50, Gradient = −0.0361):
    0.50 − (0.001 × (−0.0361)) = 0.50 + 0.0000361 = 0.500036
    • For h₂→o (Old weight = 0.90, Gradient = −0.0370):
    0.90 − (0.001 × (−0.0370)) = 0.90 + 0.0000370 = 0.900037
  • Hidden Layer Connections from Input i₁ (Activation = 1.00)
    • For i₁→h₁ (Old weight = 0.10, Gradient = −0.0092):
    0.10 − (0.001 × (−0.0092)) = 0.10 + 0.0000092 = 0.100009
    • For i₁→h₂ (Old weight = 0.20, Gradient = −0.0167):
    0.20 − (0.001 × (−0.0167)) = 0.20 + 0.0000167 = 0.200017
  • Hidden Layer Connections from Input i₂ (Activation = 0.50)
    • For i₂→h₁ (Inhibitory | Old weight = −0.30, Gradient = −0.0046):
    −0.30 − (0.001 × (−0.0046)) = −0.30 + 0.0000046 = −0.299995
    • For i₂→h₂ (Inhibitory | Old weight = −0.40, Gradient = −0.0083):
    −0.40 − (0.001 × (−0.0083)) = −0.40 + 0.0000083 = −0.399992

Notice what happened to i₂’s inhibitory connections: they started at negative values (−0.30 and −0.40), but their gradients are also negative. Gradient descent therefore subtracts a negative quantity, making the weights slightly less negative: −0.299995 and −0.399992. That makes sense here: the network needs a larger output, so it should weaken the inhibitory influence of i₂ as well as strengthen the positive connections. The sign of a weight by itself does not determine whether it should become more or less inhibitory; the gradient determines the direction of the update.

Also notice just how small these steps were: every weight barely moved. That is normal, not a mistake — with a learning rate this size, no single update is meant to move a weight very far on its own. A real network repeats this entire six-step process across thousands of training examples, often for many passes through the whole training set, and it's the accumulation of many tiny, carefully-sized nudges — not one big correction — that gradually teaches it.

the payoff
Run the forward pass again with these updated weights and the prediction creeps a little closer to 1.00 (about 0.66681 in this worked example). Repeat this entire six-step dance for every training example, thousands of times over, and that's the basic pattern of how a network learns.

Important qualifications

While walking through the classic sigmoid function is the gold standard for learning how a network assigns blame, it is vital to understand how these concepts apply to modern AI architectures like Large Language Models (LLMs).

1. The Deep Learning Revolution: ReLU

Because the sigmoid curve flattens out at its extremes, its derivative drops toward zero. Across many layers, repeatedly multiplying by derivatives smaller than 1 can contribute to the vanishing-gradient problem. ReLU became important partly because its derivative is 1 on the positive side, avoiding sigmoid saturation there and making gradient propagation easier in deep networks. ReLU does not eliminate every cause of vanishing or unstable gradients.

Instead of squashing numbers into an S-shaped curve, ReLU uses an incredibly simple threshold rule: if the input is negative or zero, turn it into 0; if the input is positive, leave it exactly as it is. Mathematically, it is written as:

Output = max(0, Input)

To appreciate why ReLU revolutionized deep learning, we can directly compare its slope (sensitivity) to the sigmoid function across different input zones:

Input Type Sigmoid Slope ReLU Slope Impact on Error Signal
Highly Positive (e.g., +10) Near 0 (flat plateau) 1 No local shrinking. On the positive side, the derivative is 1, so this operation does not attenuate the gradient.
Near Zero (e.g., 0) Maxes out at 0.25 1 Derivative = 1. At positive inputs, ReLU passes the local gradient without attenuation.
Negative (e.g., -5) Near 0 (flat plateau) 0 Shut Down. The neuron turns off completely, and no error passes through it.

Because the slope of ReLU for any positive number is exactly 1, the backpropagation math simplifies to Weight Change = Raw Error × 1 = Raw Error. The error signal does not shrink, degrade, or vanish, allowing modern architectures to be hundreds of layers deep without training grinding to a halt.

The One Flaw: "Dead ReLU" and the Leaky Fix

While ReLU avoids sigmoid-style saturation on the positive side, it creates a different problem on the negative side. If a neuron's input becomes consistently negative, its output stays at 0 and its slope hits a hard 0. Because a slope of 0 completely destroys the error signal (Error × 0 = 0), that neuron stops updating its weights entirely. It becomes "dead."

To fix this, researchers created variants like Leaky ReLU. Instead of dropping to an absolute zero for negative numbers, Leaky ReLU assigns them a tiny, flat slope (like 0.01). This gives the negative side a small nonzero derivative, which can allow some gradient to pass and reduce the chance of a unit remaining permanently inactive.

2. The Modern Role of Sigmoid in LLMs

The sigmoid function remains useful; its role has expanded beyond being the default hidden-layer activation. Instead of acting as a standalone hidden layer activation, it has been repurposed into highly specialized, modern operations:

  • Self-Gating (SwiGLU): Some modern LLMs use SwiGLU, a gated feed-forward architecture in which one projection is transformed with SiLU (also called Swish) and then multiplied elementwise by another projection. SiLU is defined as x × sigmoid(x). Its derivative approaches 1 for large positive inputs, rather than being driven toward 0 as the sigmoid derivative is.
  • Memory-Saving Losses (SigLIP): SigLIP uses a sigmoid-based loss for image-text matching rather than a softmax contrastive loss. Image-text pairs are scored independently, which avoids the need to form the same global softmax normalization over all pairwise similarities in a batch.
  • Classification Outputs: Sigmoid remains the final step when fine-tuning an LLM for binary tasks, compressing raw logits into a neat 0% to 100% probability for safety or toxicity text filtering.

3. Forward Pass Governs the Backward Pass

Backpropagation computes derivatives through the mathematical operations used in the forward pass. Each operation contributes its local derivative to the chain of derivatives that carries the loss signal backward. Thus, when a model uses a particular nonlinear operation such as sigmoid or SiLU in its forward computation, backpropagation uses the corresponding derivative when calculating gradients through that operation.

Note: While the geometric shapes of activation functions change across architectures to optimize performance, the foundational "blame chain" mechanism is identical. The general pattern remains: a model computes a loss, backpropagation computes gradients through the operations that produced that loss, and an optimizer uses those gradients to update parameters.
