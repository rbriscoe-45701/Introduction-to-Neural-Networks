Backpropagation: a step-by-step tutorial for beginners

The Blame Chain

A network makes a bad guess. Before it can improve, it has to figure out exactly which of its internal connections deserve the blame and by how much. Here is that process—call backpropagation (or backward propagation of errors) — clearly presented one step at a time.

Picture a very small chain of command: two input units at the start, two hidden units in the middle, and one output unit at the end that makes the final call. A signal flows forward through this chain, and out comes a prediction.

Now suppose the prediction is wrong. There is a gap between the network's desired output and it actual output. The only way to find out where responsibility lies for the mistake is to start at the end, where it is visible, and work backward, asking each unit in turn: how much did you contribute to the error? That backward hand-off of blame, one layer at a time, is the essence of backpropagation.

Below, we'll run that process on one tiny, fully-labeled network, using only multiplication and subtraction. No calculus background is needed to follow along — wherever a slope or a rate of change would normally require it, we've pre-derived the simple formula for you, so every number is checkable on a phone calculator.

Six words you'll need
weight
A number sitting on a connection between two units, loosely modeled on the strength of a biological synapse. See “what a weight really is,” just below.
activation
The number a unit reports after squashing its raw input through the sigmoid function — always between 0 and 1. See “activation and the sigmoid function,” just below.
error
The gap between what the network predicted and what the true answer actually was.
sensitivity (slope)
How steep a unit's S-shaped output curve is exactly where that unit currently sits. Every error signal passing through a unit gets multiplied by this — steep means the signal counts in full, flat means the unit barely reacts to it.
gradient
The exact amount, and direction, to nudge one weight so the network's error shrinks a little.
learning rate
A dial controlling how big a step the network takes when it applies a gradient. We'll use 0.001, a commonly used default.

A closer look: what a weight really is

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) are conceptually and mathematically modeled after the interconnected networks of biological neurons found in the human brain. In computer science, we simulate this biology using artificial "units" (neurons) that process data, "weights" (synapses) that scale the signal, and "activations" (electrical impulses) that travel between layers. To understand how information and "blame" flow through these artificial systems, it helps to look at their natural inspiration.

Figure 2: Computational model of an Artificial Neural Network.

Real neurons connect to each other through synapses, and how much activity in one neuron (the presynaptic neuron, the one sending the signal) affects the neuron on the receiving end (the postsynaptic neuron) depends on the strength and type of that synapse. Some synapses are excitatory: activity in the presynaptic neuron pushes the postsynaptic neuron toward firing. Others are inhibitory: activity in the presynaptic neuron pushes the postsynaptic neuron away from firing, damping it down instead.

A weight in this network is a simplified stand-in for that synaptic strength. A positive weight acts like an excitatory synapse — the more active the sending unit is, the more it pushes the receiving unit toward turning on. A negative weight acts like an inhibitory synapse — the more active the sending unit is, the more it pushes the receiving unit toward turning off. A weight near zero barely functions as a synapse at all: however active the sending unit gets, almost none of that activity reaches the receiving unit.

Our example network mixes both kinds. i₁'s connections into the hidden layer (weights 0.10 and 0.20) are excitatory: the more active i₁ is, the more it pushes h₁ and h₂ toward firing. i₂'s connections into the hidden layer (weights −0.30 and −0.40) are inhibitory instead — activity in i₂ actually pushes both hidden units away from firing, damping them down rather than encouraging them. The two connections from the hidden layer into the output (weights 0.50 and 0.90) are both excitatory, with h₂'s connection (0.90) a much stronger push than h₁'s (0.50), the same way a stronger real synapse drives its target neuron more forcefully. When later steps talk about “correcting a weight,” this is literally what's being adjusted: exactly how excitatory — or, as with i₂'s connections, how inhibitory — a particular connection should be, given the mistake the network just made.

A closer look: activation and the sigmoid function

Real neurons don't report a smooth number between 0 and 1 — they either fire an electrical spike or they don't, and what varies is how often. Each unit in this network is a simplified stand-in for that: instead of a firing rate, it reports one number, its activation, somewhere between 0 (completely silent) and 1 (firing as hard as it can).

To get that number, a unit first adds up everything arriving at it — every incoming unit's activation, multiplied by the weight of that particular connection — into one raw total. Then it runs that raw total through a fixed squashing rule called the sigmoid function, written sigmoid(x) = 1 ÷ (1 + e−x), where x stands for whatever raw total is being squashed. The sigmoid function takes any number at all, however large or however negative, and maps it onto a value strictly between 0 and 1. That's the S-shaped curve below. In the example, the unit's raw total input is 1.50 and its output (activation) after passing through the sigmoid function is .82

the math — computing one activation
sigmoid(x)=1 ÷ (1 + e−x)
raw input (weighted sum)=1.50
e−1.50≈0.2231
1 + 0.2231=1.2231
1 ÷ 1.2231=0.82 ← the activation

e here is just a fixed constant, about 2.718; e−x is a single button (often labeled ex) on any scientific calculator, or one search away on your phone. You won't need to run this calculation again — everywhere else in this walkthrough, we're simply handed each unit's already-computed activation (0.60, 0.80, 0.23, and so on) and work forward from there. This is just so you've seen, once, where numbers like that actually come from.

Notice how the curve behaves. Near the middle, it's steep: a small change in raw input produces a big swing in activation. Toward either end, it flattens: even a large change in raw input barely moves the activation at all, because the unit is already close to fully off or fully on. That difference in steepness from point to point is exactly what we'll call a unit's sensitivity, starting in Step 2 — how much a unit's activation would actually respond, right now, to a change in what's feeding into it.

Meet the network

Here is our cast, mid-training. A forward pass has already run once — the numbers below are simply what came out of it. The two input values are given facts about the example (say, two measurements from a photo); the hidden and output activations are what the network computed from them, each by squashing its own raw input through the sigmoid function above. The blue numbers are all known before we start; nothing here is a mystery yet.

This example's target is 1.00. Suppose the task is “does this photo contain a face” — a target of 1.00 means a face really is present in this training photo, confirmed by whoever labeled the data; a target of 0.00 would mean a face is confirmed absent. The network's job is to eventually output something close to 1.00 whenever a face really is there, and something close to 0.00 whenever it isn't. Right now, on this particular example, it's badly off: it output 0.23 when it should have output something close to 1.00.

a note on biases
Real units usually carry one more adjustable number besides their incoming weights: a bias, added to the raw input before it's squashed through the sigmoid function. You can think of a bias as a a constant numerical value added to the input of a unit that does not change as the result of incoming activations. We've left biases out of this walkthrough to keep the arithmetic to a single, easy-to-follow pipeline, but they aren't handled any differently: each unit's bias gets updated using that same unit's error —represented using the Greek letter delta (δ) — exactly like every weight we compute below, just without an incoming activation to multiply by.
1

Calculate the output error

Everything starts at the very end, because that's the only place the network can check its work. It compares its final guess to the true target.

Our output unit predicted 0.23 — you can read that as roughly a 23% chance, in the network's own estimation, that a face is present. The target was 1.00: a face is, in fact, present, with certainty. Subtracting the two gives the error: 1.00 − 0.23 = 0.77. This subtraction happens at the output unit specifically, because the output unit is the only place in the network where we have both a prediction and a known correct answer to compare it against — the hidden and input units have no such comparison available to them.

the math
target−prediction=error
1.00−0.23=0.77
2

Fix the connections into the output

Before going any deeper into the network, we fix the two connections that feed directly into the output unit — the ones from h₁ and h₂. But the raw error from Step 1 (0.77) isn't quite ready to use on its own, and here's why.

Recall from the sigmoid function, above: every unit's activation comes from squashing its raw input through that same S-shaped curve, and the curve isn't equally steep everywhere. The output unit's activation, 0.23, sits at one particular point on that curve, pictured again below. How steep the curve is exactly at that point tells us how much this unit's activation would actually change in response to a change in the weights feeding into it — information we need before the raw error can be turned into a sensible correction. Think of the sigmoid curve as like a hill. In the middle (activation = 0.5), the hill is a steep cliff. A tiny step forward or backward changes your altitude dramatically. At the edges (near 0 or 1), the hill flattens out into a plateau. Taking a step forward or backward barely changes your altitude at all. If a curve has a high slope (steep cliff), the neuron is highly sensitive. A tiny change in input causes a large jump in output. If a curve has a low slope (flat plateau), the neuron has low sensitivity. Changing the inputs will barely move the output at all.

So, “sensitivity” here just means: how steep is this curve exactly at the point where our unit happens to be sitting? The curve is steepest at its midpoint — output 0.5 — where the slope hits its highest possible value, 0.25. Our output landed at 0.23 instead, off to one side of that midpoint. That 0.23 is already the activation — a point already sitting on the curve, not a raw number waiting to be squashed — so we can find the slope there directly. The sigmoid curve has a convenient property: its slope at any point can be worked out entirely from the curve's own output at that point, using activation × (1 − activation), with no need for calculus or for running anything back through the sigmoid function again. So the slope where our output sits is 0.23 × (1 − 0.23) = 0.23 × 0.77 = 0.1771. That number — the slope right at 0.23, not at 0.5 — tells us how much this unit's activation would actually move if we adjusted the weights feeding into it. A unit sitting further from the steep middle resists being corrected a little, even when it's badly wrong — which is exactly what's happening here: our output's slope (0.1771) is well below the peak of 0.25, so it will shrink the raw error down before any correction gets computed.

So, we multiply the raw error, 0.77, by the output unit's own slope, 0.1771: 0.77 × 0.1771 = 0.1364. That combined number is the output's sensitivity-adjusted error — call it δo — and every later calculation in this walkthrough builds on this number, 0.1364, rather than on the raw 0.77. From δo, we compute a separate correction for each of the two connections feeding into the output — one for the connection coming from h₁, a second, separate one for the connection coming from h₂ — by multiplying δo by the activation of whichever hidden unit sent that particular connection's signal.

It's worth being precise about what this “correction” actually is. It is not the new weight itself, and it is not a percentage change. It is called a weight gradient, and it packs two crucial pieces of information into a single number:

  • The Sign (Direction): The gradient points in the direction that increases error. Therefore, a positive gradient means increasing the weight makes the mistake worse, so we will eventually nudge the weight down. A negative gradient means the opposite.
  • The Magnitude (Scale): Its size tells us roughly how much that particular weight matters to the current mistake, relative to every other weight being corrected in this same pass.

Concretely, we calculate a separate gradient for each connection tracing back to the output unit:

  • For the connection from h₁: δo × h₁'s activation = 0.1364 × 0.60 = 0.0818
  • For the connection from h₂: δo × h₂'s activation = 0.1364 × 0.80 = 0.1091

These two separate computations share the same δo simply because both connections feed into the same output unit's error. Nothing gets applied yet, though—every gradient in the network must be computed first across all six steps. Only in Step 6 do we actually use them to update the weights.

the math — the output's own sensitivity, then its adjusted error
0.23 × (1−0.23)=slope of o=0.1771
erroro × slope of o=δ₀
0.77×0.1771=0.1364
the math — now the two weight gradients
δ₀×activation of h₁=gradient for (h₁→o)
0.1364×0.60=0.0818
δ₀×activation of h₂=gradient for (h₂→o)
0.1364×0.80=0.1091
3

Send the error back to the hidden layer

This is the heart of backpropagation. The hidden units never saw a "true target" — they have nothing to compare themselves against. So we can't compute their error the way we did in Step 1. Instead, we work out how much each hidden unit is responsible for the output's mistake, based entirely on the strength of its connection to the output.

We take δ₀, the output's adjusted error from Step 2 (the one that already has the output's own sensitivity folded in — not the raw 0.77 from Step 1), and multiply it by the weight of the connection linking each hidden unit to the output: for h₁, δ₀ × weight(h₁→o) = 0.1364 × 0.50 = 0.0682; for h₂, δ₀ × weight(h₂→o) = 0.1364 × 0.90 = 0.1227. This mirrors the forward pass in reverse: going forward, a hidden unit's activation reached the output multiplied by exactly this same weight, so running that same multiplication backward is a fair way to decide how much of the output's mistake traces back to that hidden unit. A hidden unit with a strong connection to a badly-wrong output is assigned a large share of the error — as h₂ is here, with 0.1227 against h₁'s 0.0682. A hidden unit with a weak or near-zero connection contributed almost nothing to that mistake in the forward pass — and is assigned almost none of it back now.

the math
δ₀×weight (h₁→o)=error received by h₁
0.1364×0.50=0.0682
δ₀×weight (h₂→o)=error received by h₂
0.1364×0.90=0.1227
4

Account for each hidden unit's sensitivity

Being assigned a share of the error doesn't automatically mean a unit is easy to correct. Each hidden unit sits on that same sigmoid curve we've been using all along, just at its own particular point on it — h₁ at 0.60, h₂ at 0.80. Using the same slope shortcut as before, h₁'s slope is 0.60 × (1 − 0.60) = 0.24, and h₂'s slope is 0.80 × (1 − 0.80) = 0.16. Where the curve is steep, a small nudge to the incoming weights produces a big change in the unit's activation. Where the curve is flat — the unit is pinned near 0 or near 1, fully "on" or fully "off" — nudging its weights barely moves anything.

Put another way: the error we computed in Step 3 tells us how much a hidden unit's activation should change to help fix the output's mistake. The slope we compute here tells us how much that activation would actually change in response to a change in the unit's own inputs.

Multiplying the two together—exactly the trick we already used in Step 2 on the output unit—combines “how much should this change” with “how much would this actually change” into one number. Using that same shortcut, we get each hidden unit's final, sensitivity-adjusted error:

  • For h₁: 0.0682 × 0.24 = 0.0164
  • For h₂: 0.1227 × 0.16 = 0.0196

Notice what happened here: h₂ was assigned a much larger raw error (0.1227 vs. 0.0682), but it is also more "maxed out"—sitting at 0.80, closer to its ceiling—so its slope (0.16) is flatter than h₁'s (0.24). After sensitivity is factored in, the gap between them narrows dramatically to 0.0196 vs. 0.0164. A unit that's pinned to one extreme is hard to move, no matter how large an error it's carrying.

the math — slope, then sensitivity-adjusted error
0.60 × (1−0.60)=slope of h₁=0.24
0.0682 × 0.24=0.0164(h₁'s adjusted error)
0.80 × (1−0.80)=slope of h₂=0.16
0.1227 × 0.16=0.0196(h₂'s adjusted error)
5

Fix the connections into the hidden layer

We finally reach the first set of connections in the network — the ones running from the two input units into the two hidden units. The pattern is exactly the one from Step 2, just one layer further back: multiply each hidden unit's sensitivity-adjusted error by the activation of the input unit feeding that connection. For h₁, whose sensitivity-adjusted error was 0.0164: the gradient for i₁→h₁ is 0.0164 × 1.00 = 0.0164, and the gradient for i₂→h₁ is 0.0164 × 0.50 = 0.0082. For h₂, whose sensitivity-adjusted error was 0.0196: the gradient for i₁→h₂ is 0.0196 × 1.00 = 0.0196, and the gradient for i₂→h₂ is 0.0196 × 0.50 = 0.0098.

The logic is the same as it was in Step 2: a connection can only be corrected in proportion to how much signal was actually flowing through it. If an input unit's activation were 0, it would have sent no signal down that connection at all during the forward pass — so no matter how large or small the connecting weight happens to be, that connection played no part in this particular mistake, and its gradient correctly comes out as 0 regardless of the weight. An input unit with a strong activation, by contrast, sent a strong signal down every connection leading out of it, so those connections get a correspondingly larger correction.

the math
adj. error h₁×activation i₁=gradient (i₁→h₁)
0.0164×1.00=0.0164
adj. error h₁×activation i₂=gradient (i₂→h₁)
0.0164×0.50=0.0082
adj. error h₂×activation i₁=gradient (i₁→h₂)
0.0196×1.00=0.0196
adj. error h₂×activation i₂=gradient (i₂→h₂)
0.0196×0.50=0.0098
6

Take the collective step

The chain reaction has now reached the beginning of the network. Every single connection — all six in our toy example, or all 328,320 in a real one — has an exact number attached to it: precisely how much, and in which direction, that one connection should change to make the network's error a little smaller.

Only now does gradient descent actually do anything. It takes every one of those numbers, scales it by the learning rate (our dial, set to 0.001, a commonly used default), and nudges every weight in the entire network at the same time. Nothing moves before this point; nothing is skipped after it. Take the connection from h₁ to the output: its old weight was 0.50, and its gradient (from Step 2) was 0.0818, so its new weight is 0.50 + (0.001 × 0.0818) = 0.50 + 0.0000818 = 0.500082. Every other weight in the network updates the same way, each using the old weight and gradient that belong to that particular connection.

One thing worth flagging if you ever see backpropagation written out in code (in a library like PyTorch, for instance): it's usually written as subtracting the scaled gradient, weight = weight − learning_rate × gradient, not adding it as we've done here. That's not a contradiction — it's the identical idea with the sign bookkeeping handled differently. We built every δ and every gradient in this walkthrough starting from a positive error, target minus prediction, so a bigger mistake always produces a bigger positive correction, and adding that correction moves each weight the right way. Code libraries typically define things the other way around — prediction minus target, then the raw calculus derivative of the loss — which comes out negative exactly when the network needs to increase its output, so they subtract instead to end up moving in the same direction. Same math, opposite bookkeeping.

Why scale the gradient down at all, instead of just adding it straight onto each weight? Because a gradient is only trustworthy for a small step. It's calculated from the network's current state, and if that state changes too much in one move, the gradient's advice is no longer accurate — it was describing a network that no longer exists. Multiplying every gradient by a small learning rate keeps each update conservative: enough to make steady, real progress on the error, but not so much that the network overshoots a good solution or lurches somewhere worse. This is also where the name comes from — the network is descending toward lower error in small, controlled steps, not leaping there in one.

Notice just how small that step was: the weight barely moved, from 0.50 to 0.500082. That's normal, not a mistake — with a learning rate this size, no single update is meant to move a weight very far on its own. A real network repeats this entire six-step process across thousands of training examples, often for many passes through the whole training set, and it's the accumulation of many tiny, carefully-sized nudges — not one big correction — that gradually teaches it. (A much larger learning rate, like the 0.5 used in an earlier version of this walkthrough, makes the arithmetic easier to see by hand, but in real training it would usually make learning unstable.)

The same update rule applies whether a connection is excitatory or inhibitory — there's nothing special to do differently. Take i₂’s inhibitory connection into h₁, weight −0.30: its gradient (from Step 5) was 0.0082, so its new weight is −0.30 + (0.001 × 0.0082) = −0.30 + 0.0000082 = −0.299992. It still moved up, exactly as the sign of its gradient said it should — but because it started out negative, moving up means becoming slightly less inhibitory, not more excitatory. A single tiny step never flips a connection's fundamental character; i₂ still damps down both hidden units afterward, just a hair less forcefully.
